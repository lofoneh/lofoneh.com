i graduated from KNUST in November 2022 with a degree in metallurgical engineering.

most of my classmates were already lined up for graduate trainee roles at mining companies and steel plants. i wasn't. for the entire four years of school i'd known, somewhere quietly in the back of my head, that i wasn't going to be a metallurgical engineer.

i don't remember the exact moment i decided to learn to code. it wasn't a single moment. it was more like a series of small frustrations — textbooks that felt finished, problems that had been solved decades ago, projects that didn't ship anywhere — and then one weekend i installed Python and started a tutorial. then another. then i bought a slightly nicer keyboard.

the next fifteen months were quiet. i did a lot of tutorials. i built things i didn't finish. i finished things that didn't matter. i read so many "from zero to X" guides i could probably write one. i picked up Go because the internet said it was good for systems work. i poked at AWS, at Docker, at Linux, at all the parts of "software" that nobody had taught me in metallurgy classes.

around mid-2023 something clicked. i'd been mostly trying to build apps — frontends and backends — but the part i kept enjoying was the wiring underneath. how does the app get to the server? how do you know it's healthy? what happens when it falls over at 3am? that was the question that felt like _my_ question.

DevOps and infrastructure work — CI/CD, observability, the boring important layers — was where i wanted to be.

in February 2024 i landed a DevOps engineering internship at Adaptive Computer Solutions in Accra. six months later i was full-time. i've been there since, building pipelines, standing up observability, and answering "what changed?" at increasingly varied levels of panic.

the non-CS background still shows up in my work, and almost always for the better. metallurgy taught me to think about systems that fail in messy, real-world ways — fatigue, stress, slow degradation — and that mindset is _exactly_ right for production infrastructure. computers are also messy real-world systems pretending to be clean ones. patient debugging is the same skill no matter what's breaking.

i still think about the long way around. the fifteen months in 2022 and 2023 where i wasn't anywhere, doing tutorials in my bedroom, not sure if any of it would lead anywhere. those months were lonely. they were also, in retrospect, the most important investment i've made.

i'm not going to tell you it was efficient. i'm just going to say it was honest.

and that, in the end, is what i want from the systems i build too.
